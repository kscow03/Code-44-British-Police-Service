Module 8

Armed Vehicle Tactics

ARU Academy — Student Learning & Reference

Operational Training

Module Overview

Armed incidents involving vehicles can develop rapidly and require effective coordination between ARU crews, Roads Policing and other supporting resources.

Module 8 introduces the principles surrounding armed vehicle incidents, vehicle containment and coordinated tactical vehicle operations.

This page is designed both as pre-learning and as an operational refresher that officers can return to after qualification.

Practical tactics, exact positioning and current server mechanics are demonstrated and assessed by approved ARU trainers.

1. Learning Objectives

Vehicle Incidents Understand the additional considerations created when an armed subject is using or occupying a vehicle.
Communication Understand the importance of clear communication between crews during coordinated vehicle operations.
Roles Understand that different vehicles and officers may have different responsibilities during the same operation.
Risk Assessment Apply continuous threat and risk assessment as circumstances change.
Joint Working Understand the relationship between ARU and Roads Policing.
Accountability Understand the need to explain and justify decisions made during vehicle operations.

2. Armed Vehicle Incidents

Vehicles can significantly alter an armed incident.

They can allow subjects to move quickly between locations, create additional risks to members of the public and require several police resources to operate together.

ARU officers should not treat every armed vehicle incident in the same way.

The response should be influenced by:

  • The information available.
  • Whether a weapon is reported or confirmed.
  • Subject behaviour.
  • Vehicle behaviour.
  • Location.
  • Public presence.
  • Available police resources.
  • The threat posed to officers and others.
Key Principle The situation is dynamic. The plan that was appropriate when the vehicle was first located may not remain appropriate five minutes later.

3. Deployment Information

Before becoming involved in an armed vehicle incident, crews should develop the clearest possible understanding of why the vehicle is being sought and what risk is believed to exist.

Relevant information may include:

  • Why the vehicle is being sought.
  • Whether firearms have been reported or confirmed.
  • The number of occupants believed to be involved.
  • Descriptions of subjects.
  • Vehicle make, model, colour and registration where known.
  • Current location.
  • Direction of travel.
  • Behaviour of the vehicle and occupants.
  • Previous intelligence relevant to the incident.
  • Risks to officers or the public.
  • Other police resources already involved.

Officers must distinguish between reported information and confirmed information.

4. ARV Crew Coordination

Armed vehicle tactics rely upon coordinated teamwork.

Officers acting independently without understanding the wider plan can create confusion and increase risk.

ARV crews should remain aware of:

  • The location of other police vehicles.
  • The location and movement of the subject vehicle.
  • Instructions communicated over the radio.
  • The responsibilities assigned to their crew.
  • Changes in subject behaviour.
  • Changes in the threat.
  • Members of the public and civilian vehicles nearby.
Remember Do not become so focused on the subject vehicle that awareness of the wider incident is lost.

5. Communication

Vehicle incidents can change quickly. Relevant information must therefore be passed between participating crews clearly and promptly.

Useful updates may include:

  • Current vehicle location.
  • Direction of travel.
  • Changes in speed or behaviour.
  • Number of occupants.
  • Confirmed or suspected weapons.
  • Changes in threat.
  • Changes to the tactical plan.
  • Requests for additional resources.
  • Loss of visual contact.
  • Vehicle stopping or occupants leaving the vehicle.

Radio transmissions should remain clear, relevant and concise.

6. Roads Policing Integration

Armed vehicle incidents may involve both ARU and Roads Policing resources.

The two capabilities should complement one another rather than operate as separate responses.

Roads Policing may support vehicle management, pursuit activity, traffic control and specialist driving capability, while ARU provides the armed capability required to manage the firearms threat.

Officers must understand which unit or commander is coordinating each part of the incident.

7. Understanding the Different Vehicle Responses

TPAC, hard stops and smash-and-grab interventions are not interchangeable terms.

TPAC Primarily concerns the coordinated management and containment of the moving vehicle phase.
Hard Stop A coordinated high-risk intervention intended to bring a vehicle and its occupants under police control.
Smash & Grab A rapid coordinated intervention used where the circumstances and authorised tactical plan support it.
Important The tactic should follow the threat assessment and operational objective. Officers should not select a tactic simply because it is the one they personally prefer.

8. TPAC — Code44 Procedure Overview

Tactical Pursuit and Containment may form part of the response where trained resources are available and the circumstances justify its use.

Within Code44, TPAC should be treated as a coordinated operation rather than individual vehicles acting independently.

General Process

  1. Information and Assessment
    Establish why the vehicle is being sought, what threat is believed to exist and what resources are available.
  2. Coordination
    ARU and Roads Policing establish a shared understanding of the incident and who is coordinating the vehicle phase.
  3. Roles
    Participating crews are given clear responsibilities and understand what other vehicles are expected to achieve.
  4. Vehicle Phase
    Trained units work together to control the vehicle incident in accordance with the agreed plan and current Code44 server mechanics.
  5. Transition
    Once the moving vehicle phase ends, ARU officers reassess the remaining firearms threat.
  6. Resolution
    Occupants are brought under control and the incident transitions into arrest, medical, evidence or investigation stages as appropriate.
Key Point The end of the pursuit or vehicle phase does not automatically mean the end of the firearms incident.

9. Hard Stop — Code44 Procedure Overview

A hard stop is a coordinated intervention used during appropriate high-risk vehicle incidents.

Students should understand the overall process before practical training.

Before the Stop

  • Confirm the target vehicle as far as reasonably possible.
  • Confirm why armed intervention is being considered.
  • Share relevant occupant and threat information.
  • Confirm which units are participating.
  • Establish clear responsibilities.
  • Ensure participating crews understand the current plan.

During the Intervention

  • Follow the agreed tactical plan.
  • Maintain awareness of other officers and vehicles.
  • Communicate significant changes immediately.
  • Maintain firearms discipline.
  • Avoid unnecessary individual improvisation.
  • Continually reassess the occupants and threat.

Once the Vehicle Is Controlled

  • Reassess the location and behaviour of all occupants.
  • Account for previously reported weapons.
  • Maintain communication between participating officers.
  • Respond proportionately to changing behaviour.
  • Transition into arrest and scene management when appropriate.

After the Incident

  • Confirm all occupants are accounted for.
  • Identify casualties or medical requirements.
  • Account for ARU equipment.
  • Provide relevant command and control updates.
  • Complete required reporting.
  • Participate in the operational debrief.
Practical Training Required Exact positioning, movement and server-specific execution are demonstrated by ARU trainers and should not be learned solely from written material.

10. Smash & Grab — Code44 Procedure Overview

A smash-and-grab intervention is a coordinated vehicle operation used where the circumstances and authorised tactical plan support a rapid intervention.

The tactic relies heavily upon preparation and coordination.

General Sequence

  1. Confirm the Objective
    Understand who or what the operation is intended to control.
  2. Confirm the Information
    Review vehicle, occupant, weapon and threat information.
  3. Allocate Responsibilities
    Participating officers and crews understand their assigned role.
  4. Coordinate the Intervention
    The operation occurs under a shared plan rather than through separate individual actions.
  5. Control the Occupants
    Officers reassess behaviour and manage the remaining threat once intervention begins.
  6. Transition
    Once the immediate threat is controlled, move into arrest, medical care and scene management as appropriate.
Remember Students should know their assigned role before the intervention begins rather than attempting to work it out during the operation.

11. Firearms Considerations

The presence of a suspected firearm does not remove the requirement for continuous assessment and proportional decision-making.

Officers should consider:

  • What weapon information is available.
  • Whether that information is confirmed.
  • Current subject behaviour.
  • Risk to members of the public.
  • Risk to officers.
  • Actions of other participating units.
  • Changes in the tactical plan.

If circumstances change, the assessment and police response must also be reconsidered.

Do Not Operate on Assumptions The original report is important, but officers must continue assessing what is actually happening.

12. When the Vehicle Stops

A vehicle stopping does not automatically mean the incident has ended.

Officers should remain aware that:

  • Occupants may remain inside.
  • Occupants may attempt to leave.
  • Previously unseen occupants may become visible.
  • New weapon information may become available.
  • Subject behaviour may change.
  • The threat may increase.
  • The threat may decrease.

Communication and reassessment continue until the incident has been brought under control.

13. Changing or Aborting the Plan

Tactical plans should never continue simply because they were the original plan.

A plan may need to change if:

  • The target vehicle cannot be confirmed.
  • The vehicle changes location or behaviour significantly.
  • Public risk increases.
  • Participating units are no longer in the expected position.
  • A weapon is confirmed or disproved.
  • New occupants are identified.
  • The threat reduces.
  • The threat increases.
  • A server or vehicle mechanic fails.
  • Command changes the operational objective.
Key Principle A coordinated plan that is no longer safe or suitable should be changed, paused or abandoned rather than forced through.

14. Post-Incident Accountability

Officers should be capable of explaining the decisions they made during an armed vehicle incident.

They should be able to explain:

  • What information they had.
  • What threat they believed existed.
  • What their assigned role was.
  • Why they took a particular action.
  • How circumstances changed.
  • How their decisions changed.
  • What they communicated to other officers.
  • Whether equipment or server issues affected the operation.

Honest debriefing and accountability are part of professional ARU performance.

15. Practical Training

Module 8 is primarily a practical training module.

Once pre-learning has been completed, students will undertake trainer-led exercises designed to develop communication, teamwork, decision-making and performance during armed vehicle incidents.

Practical exercises may include:

  • Simulated vehicle intelligence briefings.
  • ARV and Roads Policing coordination.
  • Developing vehicle incidents.
  • TPAC awareness exercises.
  • Hard-stop scenarios.
  • Smash-and-grab scenarios.
  • Changing threat information.
  • Vehicle stops with multiple occupants.
  • Post-incident handovers and debriefs.

Scenarios may change between sessions and students should not expect every exercise to have one fixed solution.

16. Module 8 Key Revision Points

  • Armed vehicle incidents are dynamic.
  • Continuous reassessment is required.
  • Clear communication between crews is essential.
  • Understand your role within the wider plan.
  • Maintain awareness of officers, vehicles and the public.
  • Separate reported information from confirmed information.
  • ARU and Roads Policing may operate together.
  • TPAC primarily manages the moving vehicle phase.
  • A hard stop is a coordinated high-risk vehicle intervention.
  • Smash-and-grab operations rely upon rapid coordinated intervention.
  • Practical execution is taught by trainers.
  • A vehicle stopping does not automatically end the firearms threat.
  • Plans must change when circumstances change.
  • Officers must be able to explain their decisions afterwards.

Student Reminder

Reading this module does not qualify a student to independently conduct or instruct armed vehicle tactics.

Students must complete trainer-led practical training and demonstrate safe, controlled and professional performance before Module 8 can be signed off.
