Trainer Module 6 — Firearms Fundamentals & Operational Readiness

Instructor Lesson Plan

ARU Academy • Cohort 001

Trainer-Only Resource

Lesson Overview

Module 6 develops the firearms-safety standards introduced during Module 5 and begins preparing students to operate responsibly as armed officers within Code44.

The lesson focuses on operational readiness, communication, team awareness, decision-making, server mechanics and equipment accountability.

Trainers should assess whether students can combine the learning from Modules 1–5 while operating within controlled FiveM simulations.

Lesson Module 6 — Firearms Fundamentals & Operational Readiness
Recommended Duration 90–120 minutes
Current Group Two ARU students
Delivery Style Briefing, supervised practical exercises and debrief
Assessment Continuous observation and knowledge check
Pass Standard Satisfactory practical performance and 80% quiz score

Mandatory Training Boundary

This lesson is designed for Code44 FiveM roleplay and simulated training only.

Trainers should focus on server mechanics, communication, judgement, teamwork, safety and accountability.

This lesson must not be presented as real-world firearms instruction.

If a student becomes confused, ignores a safety command or acts outside the agreed exercise boundaries, the exercise must be stopped.

Learning Outcomes

By the end of the lesson, each student should be able to:

  • Explain operational readiness within Code44 ARU.
  • Prepare appropriately for a trainer-led simulated deployment.
  • Identify the standard roles of the P226 and SIG MCX.
  • Explain why specialist equipment is not used automatically.
  • Communicate clearly with their ARU partner.
  • Maintain awareness of other officers and roleplayers.
  • Separate known, reported, observed and suspected information.
  • Recognise changes in subject behaviour and threat.
  • Reassess an initial decision when circumstances change.
  • Use Code44 server mechanics sufficiently for later scenarios.
  • Respond correctly when equipment or scripts fail.
  • Maintain equipment and firearms accountability.
  • Participate constructively in a debrief.
  • Accept and apply trainer feedback.

Opening Script

Suggested wording

“Module 6 moves beyond basic safety and begins preparing you to operate responsibly as an armed officer within Code44.

You will be assessed on much more than the server's weapon mechanics. Trainers will be watching your communication, judgement, awareness, teamwork and ability to respond when circumstances change.

This is not a speed exercise and it is not a competition.

Control, communication and good decisions matter more than trying to look experienced.”

Stage 1 — Operational Readiness

Explain that operational readiness is a combination of equipment, information, mindset, communication and teamwork.

Equipment Required equipment is available, accounted for and appropriate for the exercise.
Information The crew understands what has been reported and identifies what remains unknown.
Communication Crew members share important information clearly.
Awareness Officers remain aware of their partner, other units and roleplayers.
Professionalism Students remain controlled and follow trainer direction.
Decision-Making Actions are based on the circumstances within the scenario.

Operational-Readiness Discussion

Ask each student to explain what they would check or confirm before beginning an ARU training deployment.

Strong answers may include:

  • Required PPE is present.
  • Vehicle equipment is accounted for.
  • Long firearms are secured.
  • Personal sidearms are accounted for.
  • Medical equipment is available.
  • The correct radio channel is selected.
  • The crew understands the information provided.
  • Roles or responsibilities have been discussed.
  • Important information gaps are recognised.
  • The crew understands the trainer's safety boundaries.

Exercise One — Pre-Deployment Crew Brief

Give both students a short fictional incident briefing.

Ask them to conduct a brief discussion covering:

  • What has been reported.
  • What remains unknown.
  • Who may be at risk.
  • What equipment is required.
  • Which radio channel will be used.
  • How information will be shared.
  • What would cause them to stop and ask for clarification.

Trainer assessment

Look for a clear exchange of information rather than one student making every decision.

Stage 2 — Code44 Firearms Roles

SIG Sauer P226 Personal sidearm carried by trained and authorised ARU officers. Personal Sidearm
SIG MCX Primary operational carbine available for authorised deployments. Primary Carbine
SIG 716 Specialist precision capability requiring an appropriate role and authorisation. Specialist Capability
Remington Specialist breaching platform. It is not the normal primary firearm for routine ARU deployments. Breaching Capability

Discussion — Appropriate Selection

Ask both students:

Question Does being deployed to an armed incident automatically mean every officer should remove an MCX from the vehicle?

Expected learning points

  • No automatic answer applies to every scenario.
  • Selection should reflect information, role and command direction.
  • Students should not choose equipment because it looks impressive.
  • Deployment decisions should be reassessed when circumstances change.
  • The P226 remains the normal personal sidearm.
  • The SIG 716 and Remington are specialist capabilities.

Specialist Capability Boundary

Module 6 does not qualify students as precision-rifle operators or breaching specialists.

The SIG 716 and Remington may be discussed as Code44 capabilities, but detailed specialist instruction belongs within later qualification packages.

Stage 3 — Firearm Deployment and Reassessment

Explain that deployment of an ARU resource does not remove the need for continuing judgement.

Students should consider:

  • Information available at the time.
  • Subject behaviour.
  • Whether a weapon has been confirmed.
  • Risk to officers and members of the public.
  • The environment.
  • The officer's assigned role.
  • Trainer or command direction.
  • New information or changing circumstances.
Trainer Prompt What may cause an officer to reduce their level of armed intervention during a scenario?

Expected learning points

  • The subject complies.
  • A suspected weapon is disproved.
  • The immediate threat reduces.
  • New information changes the assessment.
  • Command direction changes.
  • The officer's role changes.

Stage 4 — Crew Communication

Explain that students must not assume their partner has received or understood the same information.

Useful communication includes:

  • Relevant observations.
  • Changes in subject behaviour.
  • Information received over the radio.
  • Members of the public entering the area.
  • Changes in officer positions.
  • Equipment or server problems.
  • Uncertainty or missing information.
  • Changes in the team's role.
Key teaching point Useful communication is clear, relevant and timely. Constant talking may prevent important information from being heard.

Exercise Two — Information Relay

Place students in the marked or unmarked X7.

Give each student different pieces of scenario information.

They must communicate and produce a shared understanding covering:

  • The reported incident.
  • Subject description.
  • Known risks.
  • Information gaps.
  • Who else may be involved.
  • What information should be passed over the radio.

Assess for:

  • Listening.
  • Clear summaries.
  • Avoiding unnecessary repetition.
  • Recognising conflicting information.
  • Confirming shared understanding.

Stage 5 — Radio Use

Code44 ARU normally operates on the main operational radio channel alongside other units.

A dedicated ARU channel may be used for training or operations when directed.

Students should demonstrate:

  • Listening before transmitting.
  • Clear and concise updates.
  • Correct use of the assigned channel.
  • Relevant information only.
  • Recognition of priority traffic.
  • Professional communication.

Radio Update Exercise

Provide a short developing incident and require each student to deliver a concise radio update.

The update should include:

  • Who is transmitting.
  • Current location.
  • What has been reported or observed.
  • Who may be at risk.
  • Current action.
  • Any support required.

This exercise should reinforce Module 3 APSTRA learning without turning into a lengthy speech.

Stage 6 — Team and Environmental Awareness

Explain that ARU officers must remain aware of more than the subject.

Students should monitor:

  • Their ARU partner.
  • Other officers.
  • Roleplayers.
  • Members of the public.
  • Vehicles and obstructions.
  • Doorways or access routes.
  • Changes in the training environment.
  • Radio traffic.

Trainers should actively watch for tunnel vision.

Exercise Three — Changing Officer Positions

Place students in a simple controlled scene.

During the exercise, move another authorised roleplayer or officer into a different position.

Assess whether students:

  • Notice the change.
  • Communicate the change.
  • Maintain awareness of their partner.
  • Avoid acting as though the scene is fixed.
  • Request clarification if the movement creates uncertainty.

Do not introduce complicated tactical movement. The purpose is awareness, communication and control.

Stage 7 — Classifying Information

Students should be able to separate four information categories.

Known Information established within the scenario.
Reported Information provided by another source.
Observed Information personally seen or heard by the student.
Suspected Something that may be true but has not been confirmed.

Exercise Four — Information Classification

Read the following information to the students:

Scenario Information A caller reports that a male in a dark jacket displayed a firearm near a vehicle. An officer later sees a male in a dark jacket nearby but does not see a weapon. A witness says the object may have been a phone.

Ask students to identify:

  • What is reported.
  • What has been personally observed.
  • What is known.
  • What remains suspected.
  • What further information may be required.

Expected learning points

  • The firearm report is relevant but remains reported information.
  • The matching clothing does not confirm identity.
  • No weapon has been personally observed.
  • The alternative explanation should be considered.
  • The assessment should remain flexible.

Stage 8 — Changing Threats and Reassessment

Explain that the initial assessment must not remain fixed when new information becomes available.

Students should reassess when:

  • Subject behaviour changes.
  • New information is received.
  • A suspected weapon is confirmed or disproved.
  • The risk increases or decreases.
  • Members of the public enter or leave.
  • Additional officers arrive.
  • Command direction changes.
  • The student's assigned role changes.

Scenario One — Threat Reduces

Students are responding to a report of an armed subject.

During the controlled scenario, the subject follows every instruction, keeps their hands visible and moves away from the suspected item.

Ask:

  • How has the risk changed?
  • What controls may still be required?
  • What actions may no longer be necessary?
  • What should be communicated to the partner and trainer?
  • Why is de-escalation still a successful outcome?

Expected learning points

  • Compliance may reduce the immediacy of the threat.
  • The scene still requires safe control.
  • Actions should reflect the reduced risk.
  • Communication should explain the change.
  • Force is not the measure of operational success.

Scenario Two — Threat Increases

During a controlled scenario, new information confirms a credible weapon and the subject begins behaving aggressively.

Ask:

  • What has changed?
  • Who is now at greater risk?
  • What information should be communicated?
  • Does the crew need additional support?
  • How should the earlier plan be reviewed?

Expected learning points

  • The information and behaviour have changed materially.
  • The crew must reassess immediately.
  • Relevant information should be shared promptly.
  • Support or command direction may be required.
  • The students should remain controlled rather than rushing.

Stage 9 — Code44 Server Mechanics

Trainers should ensure students understand the Code44 mechanics they will encounter in later operational scenarios.

Assess whether students can:

  • Follow trainer directions while using the relevant systems.
  • Use the appropriate controls during simulation.
  • Remain aware while interacting with equipment.
  • Recognise when an action has not worked.
  • Communicate a technical problem promptly.
  • Return to a trainer-directed safe state.

Trainers should teach the current server mechanics only. If scripts change, the lesson should be updated.

Exercise Five — Supervised Mechanics Familiarisation

Each student should complete a controlled familiarisation exercise using the current Code44 systems.

The trainer should assess:

  • Listening to instructions.
  • Controlled behaviour.
  • Communication when something does not work.
  • Awareness of the other student.
  • Response to stop or safety commands.
  • Ability to apply correction.

This exercise is about safe and reliable use of the server mechanics, not speed or competitive shooting.

Scenario Three — Script Failure

During the practical exercise, a weapon animation or inventory function does not behave as expected.

Ask:

  • Should the student continue?
  • What should be communicated?
  • Who decides whether the exercise restarts?
  • Is the technical problem automatically a student failure?

Expected learning points

  • The student should stop where appropriate.
  • The issue should be reported clearly.
  • The trainer controls the restart.
  • A technical fault is not automatically a student failure.
  • Ignoring or concealing the problem may become a training issue.

Stage 10 — Post-Exercise Accountability

The exercise does not end until equipment and students are accounted for.

Students should confirm:

  • All issued equipment is accounted for
  • Long firearms have been returned where required
  • Vehicle firearms are secured
  • Personal sidearms remain accounted for
  • Equipment or script faults have been reported
  • The training area has been checked
  • Relevant information has been passed to the trainer
  • Both students are ready for the debrief

Stage 11 — Debrief

Debrief each student individually and then as a crew.

Ask each student:

  • What information influenced your decisions?
  • What did your partner communicate effectively?
  • What changed during the scenario?
  • Did your plan change?
  • What went well?
  • What would you improve?
  • What feedback will you apply next time?

Trainers should focus on reasoning, communication and development rather than simply revealing a preferred answer.

Practical Assessment Standard

A satisfactory Module 6 performance should demonstrate:

  • Professional preparation.
  • Compliance with Module 5 safety standards.
  • Clear communication with the ARU partner.
  • Awareness of other officers and roleplayers.
  • Recognition of important information.
  • Separation of fact and assumption.
  • Recognition of changing circumstances.
  • Appropriate response to trainer commands.
  • Sufficient understanding of Code44 mechanics.
  • Honest reporting of faults or mistakes.
  • Equipment accountability.
  • Constructive participation in the debrief.

Review Questions

  1. What does operational readiness mean?
  2. How are Code44 ARVs normally crewed?
  3. What is the normal role of the P226?
  4. What is the normal role of the SIG MCX?
  5. Why are the SIG 716 and Remington treated as specialist capabilities?
  6. Does deployment to an armed incident automatically determine firearm selection?
  7. Why is crew communication important?
  8. What is the difference between known, reported, observed and suspected information?
  9. Why must officers continually reassess?
  10. What should happen if the Code44 server mechanics fail?
  11. Where must long firearms be stored when no longer required?
  12. What should happen after the practical exercise?

Expected Answers

  1. Being appropriately equipped, informed, prepared, communicative and capable of working effectively as part of the crew.
  2. Normally double-crewed by trained ARU officers.
  3. Personal sidearm carried by trained and authorised ARU officers.
  4. Primary operational carbine available for authorised deployment.
  5. The SIG 716 provides a precision capability and the Remington is a dedicated breaching platform. Both require an appropriate specialist role or authority.
  6. No. Selection depends upon information, role, circumstances and trainer or command direction.
  7. Partners may not have seen, heard or understood the same information.
  8. Known information is established, reported information comes from another source, observed information is personally seen or heard, and suspected information remains unconfirmed.
  9. Information, behaviour, risk and command direction can change.
  10. Stop where appropriate, communicate the issue and follow trainer direction.
  11. Within the designated rear firearms-storage area of the ARV.
  12. Account for and secure equipment, report problems and participate in the debrief.

Immediate Stop Criteria

Stop the exercise immediately if a student:

  • Ignores a trainer safety command.
  • Continues while confused.
  • Acts outside the agreed simulation boundary.
  • Displays repeated poor awareness.
  • Fails to communicate a serious script or equipment problem.
  • Behaves recklessly.
  • Uses specialist equipment without authority.
  • Refuses to follow trainer direction.
  • Demonstrates conduct that may compromise the other student.

Module Outcomes

Pass

The student demonstrates satisfactory safety, communication, awareness, judgement, server-mechanics competence and equipment accountability.

Pass with Development Points

The student may progress but must address limited issues identified during practical observation.

Remedial

The student requires further coaching or another controlled exercise before progressing to ARV deployment training.

Incomplete

Required reading, attendance, practical work or assessment remains outstanding.

Unsuccessful

The student has not demonstrated the required standard after appropriate training, feedback and reassessment.

Common Student Errors

  • Rushing to appear confident.
  • Allowing one student to make every decision.
  • Failing to communicate information to the partner.
  • Talking constantly and missing important radio traffic.
  • Treating reported information as confirmed fact.
  • Failing to notice changes in subject behaviour.
  • Continuing with an outdated plan.
  • Focusing only on the subject and losing team awareness.
  • Ignoring a server-mechanics problem.
  • Forgetting equipment accountability after the exercise.
  • Becoming defensive during the debrief.

Common Trainer Errors

  • Introducing hard stops, CQB or complex tactics too early.
  • Turning the exercise into a shooting competition.
  • Assessing speed rather than control and judgement.
  • Giving one student all the information.
  • Allowing one student to dominate the crew.
  • Failing to challenge unsupported assumptions.
  • Making the scenario too complicated.
  • Failing to assess both students individually.
  • Providing the expected answer before the debrief.
  • Failing to record development points.
  • Using outdated Code44 mechanics.

Closing Script

Suggested wording

“That concludes Module 6.

You have now moved beyond basic safety and started demonstrating whether you can operate responsibly as an armed officer within Code44.

The standard is not based solely on how quickly you complete a scenario or how accurately you use the server mechanics.

Communication, awareness, judgement, reassessment and accountability will remain central throughout the rest of the Academy.

Module 7 will place these skills into ARV deployments and developing armed incidents.”
