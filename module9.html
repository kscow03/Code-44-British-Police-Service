Module 9

Armed Containment, Searches & High-Risk Warrants

ARU Academy — Student Learning & Reference

Tactical Operations

Module Overview

Module 9 introduces the principles used when ARU is required to contain, search or support the arrest of potentially armed subjects.

Students will learn how containment supports incident control, how armed searches should be coordinated, how high-risk warrants differ from ordinary police activity and how ARU works alongside other policing resources.

This page provides the knowledge required before practical training and can later be used as an operational refresher.

Exact building-entry procedures, room-clearing methods, breaching techniques and current server mechanics are delivered separately by approved ARU trainers.

1. Learning Objectives

Containment Understand why containment may be established and what it is intended to achieve.
Information Identify the information required before an armed search or warrant.
Roles Understand that individual officers and teams may be given different responsibilities within the wider operation.
Communication Understand the importance of clear and controlled communication throughout the operation.
Reassessment Recognise when the threat, information or operational plan has changed.
Accountability Explain decisions and actions taken during an armed operation.

2. Armed Containment

Containment is used to help control an incident by limiting the ability of a subject or threat to move freely while officers gather information and develop an appropriate response.

It can create valuable time for:

  • Additional information to be gathered.
  • Command decisions to be made.
  • Specialist resources to arrive.
  • Members of the public to be moved away from danger.
  • Communication with subjects where appropriate.
  • A planned search or arrest to be prepared.
Key Principle Containment is not simply “standing around a building.” It is an active method of controlling risk and preserving options.

3. Purpose of Containment

Effective containment should support several operational objectives.

  • Preventing an armed subject moving into areas occupied by the public.
  • Maintaining observation where possible.
  • Creating a safer working area for specialist resources.
  • Supporting controlled communication.
  • Preventing unnecessary officers entering the immediate threat area.
  • Allowing command to understand and coordinate the incident.

The exact form of containment will depend upon the environment and the information available.

4. Information & Assessment

Before an armed containment, search or warrant progresses, officers should understand as much relevant information as possible.

Relevant information may include:

  • Why the subject is being sought.
  • Whether firearms are reported, suspected or confirmed.
  • Number and descriptions of subjects.
  • Known or suspected location of subjects.
  • Whether other persons may be inside.
  • Vulnerable persons or hostages.
  • Relevant vehicle information.
  • Known access points or environmental information.
  • Relevant intelligence or previous behaviour.
  • Available specialist resources.
  • Medical considerations.
  • Important information gaps.
Remember Intelligence may be incomplete or outdated. Students should distinguish between what has been reported, what is known and what is personally observed.

5. Roles & Responsibilities

Larger armed operations rely on officers performing coordinated roles.

The exact structure will depend on the incident, but responsibilities may include:

Firearms Command Provides operational oversight and tactical direction where required.
ARV Officers Provide the armed capability and carry out assigned operational tasks.
Containment Officers Maintain assigned observation or control responsibilities within the wider containment.
Search Team Conducts the assigned search when authorised and directed.
Response Officers May support outer scene control, witnesses, evacuations and wider policing tasks.
Specialist Resources May include NPAS, K9, medical support or other authorised capabilities.

Officers should understand their own responsibility while remaining aware of the wider operational objective.

6. Communication

Poor communication can quickly undermine an otherwise well-managed operation.

Relevant updates may include:

  • Subject sightings.
  • Movement within or around the location.
  • Changes in subject behaviour.
  • New weapon information.
  • Members of the public entering the area.
  • Changes in officer positions.
  • Loss of observation.
  • Requests for additional resources.
  • Changes in the agreed plan.
  • Medical emergencies.

Officers should communicate significant information promptly without unnecessarily flooding the radio channel.

7. Armed Searches

Armed searches may be required where officers are looking for a subject, weapon or other threat within an identified area.

Searches should be coordinated and based upon the available information.

Before a search begins, officers should understand:

  • What or who is being searched for.
  • Why an armed search is required.
  • What threat is believed to exist.
  • Which officers are involved.
  • What area is being searched.
  • What supporting resources are available.
  • How significant information will be communicated.
  • What would cause the search plan to change.
Search Discipline A search should not become a race between officers. Coordination, communication and awareness are more important than speed.

Search Principles

During trainer-led practical searches, students will be expected to demonstrate:

  • Awareness of their team.
  • Clear communication.
  • Attention to the information provided.
  • Controlled progression through the scenario.
  • Recognition of civilians or uninvolved persons.
  • Continual threat reassessment.
  • Appropriate response when new information is discovered.
  • Compliance with trainer or command direction.

Exact room-entry and clearance techniques are taught practically rather than through this student reference page.

8. High-Risk Warrants

A high-risk warrant involves circumstances where the information or threat requires specialist armed support.

ARU involvement may be required because of:

  • Known or suspected firearms.
  • Violent or high-risk subjects.
  • Previous threats towards police.
  • Intelligence suggesting increased operational risk.
  • Potential risks to occupants or members of the public.
  • The need for specialist resources or capabilities.

The existence of a warrant does not mean every operation should use the same tactical response.

The plan should reflect the information, threat, environment and objective.

9. Operational Planning

Planned operations should provide participating officers with a clear understanding of what they are expected to achieve.

A useful briefing may cover:

  • The operational objective.
  • Subject information.
  • Weapon and threat information.
  • Location information.
  • Known occupants.
  • Officer roles.
  • Available resources.
  • Communication arrangements.
  • Medical considerations.
  • Known risks and uncertainties.
  • What may cause the plan to change.
Good Briefings Create Shared Understanding Students should know what the team is trying to achieve and what their individual contribution is.

10. Specialist Resources

Containment, search and warrant operations may require support from several specialist or frontline resources.

NPAS May assist with observation, tracking and wider situational awareness.
K9 May assist with tracking, searching or other authorised specialist tasks.
Ballistic Shield May provide an additional protective capability where authorised and appropriate.
Medical Capability ARU officers may provide immediate medical support within the limitations of the scenario and available resources.
Response Officers May support cordons, evacuations, witnesses and wider scene management.
Firearms Command Coordinates tactical decision-making where required.

Specialist Equipment Is Not Automatically Required

Having access to shields, specialist firearms, K9, NPAS or other resources does not mean they must automatically be used.

Resources should support the operational objective and current risk assessment.

11. Public & Officer Safety

Containment and search operations can involve risks beyond the immediate armed subject.

Students should remain aware of:

  • Members of the public entering the area.
  • Residents or occupants who may not be involved.
  • Other officers moving within the operation.
  • Vehicle movements.
  • Medical emergencies.
  • Changes to access or escape routes.
  • Loss of communications.

Wider scene management is therefore an important part of armed operations.

12. Changing Circumstances

A tactical plan should not continue unchanged simply because it was agreed earlier.

The plan may require review if:

  • The subject changes location.
  • A weapon is confirmed or disproved.
  • Additional persons are identified.
  • A hostage or vulnerable person becomes involved.
  • The subject begins complying.
  • The subject becomes more threatening.
  • Containment is lost.
  • Public risk increases.
  • A specialist resource becomes available.
  • A server mechanic fails.
  • Command direction changes.
Key Principle A plan is a tool, not an obligation. If it no longer matches the circumstances, reassess and adapt.

13. Resolution & Handover

Once the immediate threat has been controlled, officers must transition into the next stage of the incident.

Responsibilities may include:

  • Confirming subject status.
  • Confirming whether all subjects are accounted for.
  • Identifying and securing relevant weapons or evidence within the scenario.
  • Providing or requesting medical assistance.
  • Updating Force Control and firearms command.
  • Protecting the scene.
  • Identifying witnesses.
  • Handing responsibility to relevant investigators or officers.
  • Accounting for ARU equipment.

14. Accountability

Students should be capable of explaining their actions after an operation.

They should be able to explain:

  • What information they had.
  • What threat they believed existed.
  • What their assigned role was.
  • What they personally observed.
  • What they communicated.
  • Why they made particular decisions.
  • What changed during the incident.
  • How their actions changed as a result.

Accountability does not require a perfect outcome. It requires honest, reasonable and explainable decision-making.

15. Practical Training

Module 9 contains a significant practical element.

Once the student learning has been completed, trainers may use scenarios involving:

  • Initial armed containment.
  • Developing containment incidents.
  • Search planning.
  • Armed search scenarios.
  • High-risk warrant briefings.
  • Multiple occupants.
  • Conflicting intelligence.
  • K9 or NPAS integration.
  • Ballistic shield familiarisation.
  • Changing threat information.
  • Containment failure or subject movement.
  • Post-operation handover and debrief.

Scenarios may vary and students should not expect every exercise to have one fixed solution.

16. Module 9 Key Revision Points

  • Containment is used to control risk and preserve options.
  • Containment creates time for information, command and resources.
  • Students must distinguish reported information from confirmed information.
  • Every officer should understand their assigned role.
  • Communication must remain clear and relevant.
  • Armed searches should be coordinated rather than rushed.
  • High-risk warrants require planning based on the actual threat and information.
  • Specialist resources should support the objective rather than be used automatically.
  • Public and officer safety must be considered throughout.
  • Tactical plans must be reassessed when circumstances change.
  • Resolution includes medical care, handover, scene management and equipment accountability.
  • Officers should be able to explain and justify their decisions afterwards.

Student Reminder

Reading this module does not qualify a student to independently conduct or instruct advanced building searches, breaching or specialist entry tactics.

Practical search, containment and warrant procedures must be demonstrated and assessed by approved ARU trainers.
