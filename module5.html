Module 5 — Firearms Safety and Handling

Code44 Police Service

Armed Response Unit Academy

Cohort 001

Module Overview

Module 5 begins the firearms-safety phase of the ARU Academy.

Students will learn the safety principles, responsibilities, commands and professional behaviour expected whenever firearms are present during Code44 training or operations.

This student page supports trainer-led roleplay and simulated training. It does not replace practical instruction from an approved ARU firearms trainer.

Estimated Reading Time 30–40 minutes
Lesson Delivery Trainer briefing and supervised simulation
Assessment Safety observation and knowledge check
Required Before Module 6 — Marksmanship and Weapon Management

Mandatory Safety Notice

Students must not conduct unsupervised firearms training.

All practical handling, simulated-firearms activity and scenario work must take place under the direction of an approved ARU trainer and in accordance with current Code44 server rules.

If a student is unsure, confused or loses track of an instruction, they must stop and ask the trainer for clarification.

1. Learning Objectives

After completing Module 5, you should be able to:

  • Explain why firearms safety is every officer's responsibility.
  • Describe the core safety principles used during ARU training.
  • Respond appropriately to trainer safety commands.
  • Maintain awareness of direction, surroundings and other officers.
  • Explain the importance of trigger discipline.
  • Understand the requirement for positive identification.
  • Identify the role of each Code44 ARU firearm.
  • Explain vehicle-storage and accountability requirements.
  • Communicate immediately when unsure or when a safety issue is identified.
  • Understand when and why a training exercise must be stopped.
  • Demonstrate professional behaviour during simulated training.

2. Responsibility Attached to Firearms

Firearms are specialist equipment carrying a high level of responsibility. Every trained officer remains accountable for their conduct, decisions and compliance with trainer instructions.

Students must understand that firearms safety is not only the trainer's responsibility.

Every student is expected to:

  • Listen carefully to the briefing.
  • Follow trainer instructions immediately.
  • Maintain awareness of their surroundings.
  • Speak up when something appears unsafe.
  • Stop when unsure.
  • Report mistakes honestly.
  • Avoid showing off or rushing.
  • Treat all training scenarios seriously.
  • Accept correction without argument.
  • Protect the safety of the entire group.
Core Principle A student who notices a safety concern has a responsibility to raise it, regardless of rank or experience.

3. Core Firearms-Safety Principles

The following principles apply throughout all ARU firearms training and simulated deployments.

Treat Firearms Seriously Never assume that careless handling is acceptable because the activity is taking place in a game or simulated environment.
Maintain a Safe Direction Remain aware of where the firearm is directed and who may be nearby.
Keep Clear of the Trigger Trigger contact should only occur when the exercise and trainer direction make it appropriate.
Know the Intended Target Officers must positively identify the subject and understand what may be beyond or around them.
Follow Commands Trainer and safety commands must be followed immediately.
Stop When Unsure Uncertainty should lead to a pause and clarification, not guesswork.
Maintain Control Do not rush, show off or allow excitement to replace discipline.
Report Errors Mistakes must be reported honestly so they can be corrected.

Safety Takes Priority Over Scenario Progress

A scenario can be restarted.

An assessment can be repeated.

No student should continue an exercise merely because they are worried about failing, looking inexperienced or delaying the group.

If safety, instructions or the situation become unclear, stop and request trainer guidance.

4. Trainer and Safety Commands

Trainers may use standard commands to control the lesson and maintain safety.

The exact wording may vary depending upon the exercise, but students must understand the meaning and importance of the following types of command.

Stop Immediately stop the current action and await further instruction.
Hold Position Remain where you are and avoid unnecessary movement.
Safe Return to the trainer-directed safe condition for the exercise.
Stand Down The current exercise or phase has ended.
Do Not Continue Await clarification before taking any further action.
Safety Stop The exercise has been stopped because of an actual or potential safety concern.

Students must respond immediately and must not argue, delay or continue the exercise after a safety command.

5. Safe Direction and Environmental Awareness

Students must remain aware of where a firearm is directed throughout training.

A safe direction is not simply one fixed location. It depends upon the training environment, the positions of other officers and the trainer's instructions.

Students should remain aware of:

  • Other officers.
  • Roleplayers and instructors.
  • Vehicles.
  • Walls and structures.
  • Doorways and access routes.
  • Members of the public within the scenario.
  • Changes in team position.
  • The wider environment around the intended subject.
Key Principle Moving your attention does not remove your responsibility to maintain awareness of the firearm and the people around you.

6. Trigger Discipline

Trigger discipline is a basic safety expectation throughout every firearms lesson.

Students must avoid unnecessary trigger contact and must follow the trainer's directions throughout all practical or simulated activity.

Poor trigger discipline may indicate:

  • Lack of concentration.
  • Rushing.
  • Poor awareness.
  • Failure to follow instructions.
  • Unsafe handling habits.

Repeated poor trigger discipline may result in the exercise being stopped and remedial training being required.

7. Positive Identification and Decision-Making

Firearms should never be treated as a substitute for clear observation, communication and judgement.

Before any simulated engagement decision, students should consider:

  • Who the subject is.
  • Whether the subject matches the information provided.
  • What behaviour is being displayed.
  • Whether a weapon is visible or confirmed.
  • Who else may be nearby.
  • Whether officers are moving through the area.
  • Whether communication remains possible.
  • Whether the threat has changed.
  • Whether the trainer has authorised the current phase of the exercise.
Core Principle Students must not rely solely upon assumption, clothing, location or another person's shout when making a simulated firearms decision.

8. Code44 ARU Firearms Roles

Module 4 introduced each capability. Module 5 reinforces its role within the ARU while keeping practical handling under trainer control.

SIG Sauer P226 Personal sidearm carried by trained and authorised ARU officers. Personal Sidearm
SIG MCX Primary operational carbine used during authorised ARU deployments. Primary Carbine
SIG 716 Specialist precision capability used only during authorised roles and operations. Precision Capability
Remington Shotgun Dedicated specialist breaching platform. It is not the routine primary firearm for normal ARU deployments. Breaching Platform

Remington Role Reminder

The Remington shotgun is used as a specialist breaching tool.

It must not be treated as the general-purpose primary firearm for routine deployments or ordinary building-search scenarios.

Breaching training is delivered separately to appropriately trained and authorised personnel.

9. Storage, Issue and Accountability

All firearms other than the officer's personal P226 sidearm must remain secured in the designated rear firearms-storage area of the ARV unless authorised for deployment.

This includes:

  • SIG MCX carbines.
  • SIG 716 precision rifles.
  • Remington breaching shotguns.

Students must understand that:

  • Firearms must remain accounted for.
  • Long firearms must not be left unsecured.
  • Unauthorised removal must be reported.
  • Returned firearms must be secured correctly.
  • Missing or damaged equipment must be reported immediately.
  • Responsibility continues until the item has been returned and secured.

10. Communication During Firearms Training

Clear communication reduces confusion and helps trainers identify problems before they become safety issues.

Students should communicate when:

  • They do not understand an instruction.
  • They lose sight of their assigned role.
  • Another officer enters their area.
  • A subject or roleplayer behaves unexpectedly.
  • Equipment appears faulty.
  • A training mechanic does not work as expected.
  • They believe a safety concern exists.
  • They require the exercise to stop.
Recommended Approach Communicate early, clearly and calmly. Do not wait until confusion has already affected the whole exercise.

11. Simulated Firearms Training

Code44 ARU will roleplay the use of simulation rounds and varied scenario-based exercises.

Students should treat simulated training seriously because it develops:

  • Communication.
  • Judgement.
  • Teamwork.
  • Environmental awareness.
  • Decision-making.
  • Compliance with trainer commands.
  • Professional behaviour under pressure.

Trainers may change scenarios, subject behaviour or information during an exercise.

Students should not attempt to memorise one fixed solution. They should listen, assess what is happening and respond within the instructions and safety boundaries provided.

12. Mistakes, Safety Stops and Remedial Training

Students are expected to be learning. A genuine mistake does not always mean automatic failure.

What matters is how the student responds.

A professional response includes:

  • Stopping when directed.
  • Reporting the mistake honestly.
  • Listening to the trainer.
  • Understanding what went wrong.
  • Repeating the relevant learning where required.
  • Applying the correction during the next exercise.

An exercise may be stopped because of:

  • Failure to follow a safety command.
  • Poor awareness of other officers.
  • Repeated trigger-discipline concerns.
  • Unsafe direction.
  • Confusion about the scenario.
  • Equipment or script failure.
  • Unprofessional conduct.
  • A trainer deciding that further instruction is required.
Key Principle Being stopped for correction is part of training. Ignoring or concealing a safety concern is far more serious.
