Module 11

Complex Armed Incidents & Integrated Operations

ARU Academy — Student Learning & Reference

Advanced Operational Development

Module Overview

Module 11 brings together the knowledge and practical skills developed throughout the previous Academy modules.

Complex armed incidents may involve several locations, multiple subjects, competing priorities, casualties, changing intelligence and numerous police resources operating at the same time.

Students must demonstrate that they can remain controlled and useful within a larger operation rather than becoming overwhelmed by the amount of information available.

The emphasis is on coordination, communication, command awareness, prioritisation, reassessment and integration with specialist resources.

1. Learning Objectives

Prioritisation Identify the most important risks and objectives within a complex incident.
Command Awareness Understand where the student fits within the wider command structure.
Information Management Separate important operational information from unnecessary noise.
Integration Work effectively alongside other police and specialist resources.
Reassessment Adapt when the threat, information or operational priorities change.
Accountability Explain decisions and contributions during complex operations.

2. Complex Armed Incidents

A complex armed incident may involve several problems occurring at the same time.

Examples may include:

  • Multiple armed subjects.
  • Several locations.
  • Moving vehicles.
  • Subjects entering buildings.
  • Members of the public at risk.
  • Casualties.
  • Conflicting intelligence.
  • Multiple responding police units.
  • Specialist resources.
  • Changing command priorities.
Key Principle Complexity should not remove structure. Officers should continue identifying the threat, the objective, their responsibility and the information relevant to their role.

3. Operational Priorities

During a complex incident, several tasks may appear urgent.

Students should avoid attempting to solve every problem personally.

Consider:

  • What represents the most immediate threat?
  • Who is currently at greatest risk?
  • What is ARU's immediate objective?
  • What responsibility has been assigned to your crew?
  • What other resources are already dealing with?
  • What information does command need?
  • What can safely wait?
Do Not Chase Every Problem Leaving an important assigned responsibility to deal with a less important issue may create a larger operational problem.

4. Command & Coordination

Larger armed incidents require a clear understanding of command and coordination.

Students are not expected to command the entire incident unless they have specifically been given that responsibility.

Students should understand:

  • Who is providing firearms command.
  • What their own crew has been tasked with.
  • What the wider operational objective is.
  • What information should be escalated.
  • When clarification should be requested.
  • When urgent circumstances may require immediate action.

Significant changes should be communicated to command so that the wider operational picture can be updated.

5. Information Management

Complex incidents may generate large amounts of radio traffic and intelligence.

Students should identify which information is directly relevant to:

  • The current threat.
  • The subject or subjects.
  • Their assigned task.
  • Public safety.
  • Officer safety.
  • Weapons.
  • Subject movement.
  • Casualties.
  • Changes to command direction.

Not every piece of information requires an immediate response from every officer.

Information Overload

Students should not become paralysed because several radio updates arrive at once.

Return to the basics:

  • What is my current task?
  • What has changed?
  • Does that change affect my task?
  • Does somebody else need to know?

6. Integrated Resources

Complex incidents may involve multiple capabilities working together.

ARU Armed response, containment, searches and specialist firearms support.
Response Wider scene management, witnesses, evacuations and general policing support.
Roads Policing Vehicle management, traffic control and authorised pursuit support.
NPAS Aerial observation, tracking and wider situational awareness.
K9 Search, tracking and authorised specialist support.
Medical Resources Casualty treatment and wider medical support.

Effective integrated operations depend upon each capability being used for a clear purpose.

7. NPAS Integration

NPAS may provide valuable information during complex incidents.

Within Code44, NPAS may assist with:

  • Observing subject movement.
  • Tracking vehicles.
  • Providing wider scene awareness.
  • Identifying possible routes or locations.
  • Supporting searches.
  • Providing information to ground units.

ARU officers should communicate clearly what information they need rather than expecting NPAS to solve the entire incident.

8. K9 Integration

K9 may provide additional capability during searches or subject-tracking operations.

Students should understand:

  • Why K9 is being requested.
  • What task the capability is intended to support.
  • What information the handler requires.
  • How the K9 task fits into the wider operation.
  • That other officers must continue performing their own responsibilities.
Resource Principle Request specialist resources because they solve a specific operational problem, not simply because they are available.

9. Casualties & Medical Priorities

Casualties can significantly alter an armed operation.

Students should consider:

  • Does an immediate threat remain?
  • Who requires medical assistance?
  • What medical capability is available?
  • Can medical resources access the location safely within the scenario?
  • Does another officer need to take responsibility for the casualty?
  • Does the operational plan need to change?
  • What information must be communicated?

Medical requirements should be incorporated into the wider operational picture rather than ignored until the end.

10. Multiple Subjects & Locations

Complex incidents may involve more than one subject or location.

Students must avoid assuming that controlling one person or location automatically resolves the entire incident.

Consider:

  • How many subjects were originally reported?
  • How many have been accounted for?
  • Are there outstanding vehicles?
  • Are there additional locations?
  • Has new intelligence identified another threat?
  • What responsibilities have other units been assigned?
Account for the Whole Incident One successful arrest does not automatically mean the wider threat has ended.

11. Communication

Complex incidents make disciplined communication even more important.

Communicate when:

  • A subject is located.
  • A subject moves.
  • A weapon is identified.
  • Weapon information changes.
  • A casualty occurs.
  • A containment is established or lost.
  • A vehicle becomes involved.
  • A new location becomes relevant.
  • Your task cannot be completed.
  • The current plan becomes unsuitable.

Communications should help build the operational picture rather than simply describe everything an officer sees.

12. Specialist ARU Capabilities

Code44 ARU has access to a range of specialist capabilities.

These may include:

Precision Capability SIG 716 specialist capability where appropriately authorised.
Breaching Remington specialist breaching capability.
Ballistic Shield Additional protective capability for selected operations.
Counter Terrorism Specialist operations where authorised.
VIP Protection Protective operations where required.
Specialist Access Rope access, fast-rope and other server-supported capabilities where authorised.

Awareness of these capabilities does not automatically qualify a student to perform every specialist role.

Capability Does Not Equal Necessity

Having access to a specialist capability does not mean it must be used.

Students should be able to answer:

“What operational problem are we trying to solve?”

13. Changing Operational Picture

Complex incidents rarely remain exactly as originally briefed.

The operational picture may change because:

  • A weapon is confirmed or disproved.
  • A subject moves.
  • An additional subject appears.
  • A subject is arrested.
  • A casualty occurs.
  • A hostage or vulnerable person is identified.
  • A vehicle becomes involved.
  • A containment fails.
  • NPAS provides new information.
  • K9 identifies a new search direction.
  • A server mechanic fails.
  • Command changes the operational objective.
Reassessment When the operational picture changes, ask whether your current task, assessment and plan remain appropriate.

Competing Priorities

Students may face scenarios where several legitimate priorities exist at once.

For example:

  • An armed subject remains outstanding.
  • A casualty requires treatment.
  • Members of the public require evacuation.
  • A vehicle connected to the incident begins moving.
  • Another officer requests assistance.

Students should not assume they personally need to perform every task.

Use communication, command and available resources to ensure priorities are allocated appropriately.

14. Transition & Resolution

Complex incidents often resolve gradually rather than at one single moment.

As threats are controlled, officers may need to transition from:

  • Armed response into arrest.
  • Containment into search.
  • Search into scene management.
  • Threat management into casualty care.
  • Operational response into investigation.

Students should remain aware of outstanding threats and responsibilities during every transition.

Operational Handover

A useful handover may include:

  • The original incident.
  • Subjects involved.
  • Subjects accounted for.
  • Outstanding subjects.
  • Weapon information.
  • Vehicles.
  • Locations involved.
  • Casualties.
  • Force used within the incident.
  • Outstanding risks.
  • Resources still deployed.
  • Further action required.

Post-Incident Accountability

Students should be capable of explaining:

  • What information they had.
  • What threat they believed existed.
  • What task they were assigned.
  • What they personally observed.
  • What information they communicated.
  • What decisions they made.
  • What changed during the incident.
  • How their decisions changed.
  • How they worked with other resources.
  • What they would improve.

15. Practical Training

Module 11 is predominantly scenario-based.

Students will be expected to combine knowledge from previous modules rather than learn one completely new tactical procedure.

Scenarios may include:

  • Multiple armed subjects.
  • Vehicle and building elements within the same incident.
  • Containment.
  • Search operations.
  • Multiple locations.
  • Civilians or hostages.
  • NPAS integration.
  • K9 integration.
  • Casualties.
  • Changing intelligence.
  • Specialist-resource requests.
  • Competing operational priorities.
  • Changing command objectives.
  • Post-incident handover and debrief.

What Trainers Will Assess

Module 11 is less about whether a student knows one specific procedure and more about whether they remain effective when several things happen simultaneously.

Students should demonstrate:

  • Calm decision-making.
  • Prioritisation.
  • Communication.
  • Command awareness.
  • Role discipline.
  • Accurate information handling.
  • Team awareness.
  • Appropriate use of specialist resources.
  • Recognition of changing circumstances.
  • Ability to adapt.
  • Accountability.

16. Module 11 Key Revision Points

  • Complex incidents should still be approached in a structured way.
  • Identify the immediate threat and operational objective.
  • Understand your assigned role.
  • Do not attempt to solve every problem personally.
  • Command requires relevant updates when circumstances materially change.
  • Separate useful information from unnecessary radio noise.
  • ARU should work alongside the wider police service rather than independently.
  • NPAS and K9 should be given clear operational tasks.
  • Casualties can alter operational priorities.
  • Account for multiple subjects and locations.
  • Specialist capability does not automatically mean specialist capability is required.
  • Plans and tasks must be reassessed when the situation changes.
  • Resolution may occur in several stages.
  • A clear operational handover is essential.
  • Students must be capable of explaining their decisions afterwards.

Student Reminder

Module 11 combines previous Academy learning and does not independently qualify students in specialist disciplines such as precision firearms, specialist breaching, Counter Terrorism, VIP protection, rope access or other advanced capabilities.

Specialist capabilities must be used in accordance with Code44 training, authorisation and trainer direction.
