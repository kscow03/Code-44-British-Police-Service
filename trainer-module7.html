Trainer Module 7 — ARV Deployment & Armed Incident Response

Instructor Lesson Plan

ARU Academy • Cohort 001

Trainer-Only Resource

Lesson Overview

Module 7 introduces students to deployment as part of a double-crewed Code44 Armed Response Vehicle.

Students must demonstrate that they can receive and assess deployment information, communicate as a crew, prepare while travelling, conduct an initial arrival assessment and provide clear updates during a developing armed incident.

This lesson builds upon the safety, communication, decision-making and operational-readiness standards established throughout Modules 1–6.

Lesson Module 7 — ARV Deployment & Armed Incident Response
Recommended Duration 120–150 minutes
Current Group Two ARU students
Delivery Style Briefing, ARV exercises, scenarios and debrief
Assessment Continuous practical observation and knowledge check
Pass Standard Satisfactory crew performance and 80% knowledge-check score

Mandatory Training Boundary

Module 7 is designed for Code44 FiveM roleplay and controlled scenario-based training.

This module does not qualify students in TPAC, hard stops, smash-and-grab tactics, dynamic building entry, breaching or specialist firearms roles.

Trainers must focus upon information handling, crew communication, arrival assessment, early scene control, APSTRA, command updates, reassessment and incident handover.

Any exercise must stop if a student ignores a safety command, becomes confused or acts outside the agreed scenario boundaries.

Learning Outcomes

By the end of the lesson, each student should be able to:

  • Explain the role of an Armed Response Vehicle.
  • Identify relevant information before deployment.
  • Recognise gaps or uncertainty in the initial report.
  • Conduct an effective ARV crew briefing.
  • Share deployment responsibilities between both officers.
  • Use travel time to prepare for arrival.
  • Conduct an initial assessment on arrival.
  • Identify risks to officers, subjects and members of the public.
  • Explain the purpose of early scene control.
  • Provide a concise APSTRA or radio update.
  • Request suitable resources and explain why they are needed.
  • Work appropriately with response, RPU, NPAS, K9 and medical support.
  • Recognise and respond to changing circumstances.
  • Provide a clear incident handover.
  • Participate honestly in a debrief.

Opening Script

Suggested wording

“Module 7 moves you into deployment as part of a double-crewed Armed Response Vehicle.

You will be assessed on how you receive information, communicate with your crew partner, prepare while travelling and assess the incident when you arrive.

The aim is not to reach the incident and immediately force a tactical outcome.

Good ARV officers establish what is happening, understand who is at risk, communicate clearly and use proportionate options as the incident develops.”

Stage 1 — Role of the ARV

Explain that an ARV provides a mobile armed-response capability for spontaneous and planned incidents.

ARV crews may be deployed to:

  • Reports involving firearms or suspected firearms.
  • High-risk incidents requiring trained armed support.
  • Incidents involving an immediate threat to officers or the public.
  • Developing incidents requiring early containment and assessment.
  • Planned operations authorised by firearms command.
  • Incidents requiring specialist equipment or medical capability.
Trainer Prompt Does deployment of an ARV automatically mean that force or specialist tactics will be required?

Expected learning points

  • No automatic outcome applies.
  • The crew must assess current circumstances.
  • The initial report may change.
  • Communication and proportionate decision-making remain essential.
  • A controlled resolution without force remains a successful outcome.

Stage 2 — Deployment Information

Students should identify the following before arrival:

Incident What has happened and why armed support was requested.
Persons Suspects, victims, witnesses, officers and vulnerable persons.
Location Exact location, direction of travel and relevant access information.
Weapon Whether the item is reported, described, observed, confirmed or suspected.
Behaviour What the subject is currently doing.
Risk Who may be at risk and why.
Resources Units and capabilities already available.
Unknowns Important information that has not yet been established.

Exercise One — Deployment Information Classification

Scenario Briefing A caller reports seeing a male carrying what appeared to be a handgun outside a convenience store. The male is described as wearing a dark jacket and entering a silver vehicle. Response officers are travelling, but nobody has yet confirmed the weapon or vehicle registration.

Ask students to identify:

  • What has been reported.
  • What has been confirmed.
  • What remains unknown.
  • Who may be at risk.
  • What further information they would request.
  • Which details must not be communicated as confirmed fact.

Expected learning points

  • The firearm is reported but not confirmed.
  • The vehicle and registration remain unconfirmed.
  • The subject's current location may have changed.
  • The store, public and attending officers may be at risk.
  • Students should seek updates without inventing missing information.

Stage 3 — ARV Crew Responsibilities

Explain that responsibilities may change according to the scenario, but both officers remain actively involved.

Driver Maintains safe vehicle operation, monitors updates and positions the vehicle according to the current information and direction.
Passenger / Crew Partner Supports navigation, monitors radio information, communicates updates and helps prepare the crew.
Contact Responsibility Where assigned, communicates with Force Control, other officers, witnesses or the subject.
Cover and Wider Awareness Where assigned, maintains awareness of changing risks and communicates relevant observations.

Roles should support teamwork rather than prevent either officer from raising important information.

Exercise Two — Shared Crew Briefing

Seat both students within the ARV and provide a short incident briefing.

Ask them to produce a shared crew plan covering:

  • What has been reported.
  • What remains unknown.
  • The immediate risk.
  • Current resources.
  • The radio channel in use.
  • Who will monitor communications.
  • Who will deliver the initial update.
  • What their priority will be on arrival.

Assess for:

  • Both students contributing.
  • Clear summary of information.
  • Recognition of uncertainty.
  • Shared understanding.
  • No unnecessary tactical detail before arrival.

Stage 4 — Travelling to the Incident

Explain that travel time should be used constructively.

The crew should:

  • Monitor radio updates.
  • Confirm the location.
  • Clarify the subject and vehicle description.
  • Identify whether the weapon remains reported or has been confirmed.
  • Identify officers and members of the public at risk.
  • Discuss relevant supporting resources.
  • Recognise changes in the incident.
  • Review the immediate priority for arrival.
Trainer Question What has changed since the original deployment message, and does it affect the crew's immediate priority?

Exercise Three — Developing Radio Information

Provide new pieces of information while the students are travelling or roleplaying travel to the incident.

Possible updates include:

  • The subject has changed location.
  • The vehicle description has been updated.
  • A weapon has been seen by an officer.
  • The caller has withdrawn part of the original information.
  • A vulnerable person may be involved.
  • Response officers have arrived.

Assess whether students:

  • Notice the change.
  • Share it with their partner.
  • Update their understanding.
  • Avoid treating outdated information as current.
  • Ask appropriate questions.

Stage 5 — Arrival and Initial Assessment

Students must assess the circumstances they find rather than blindly following the original report.

On arrival, students should consider:

  • Current subject location.
  • Whether the subject matches the description.
  • Whether a weapon is visible or confirmed.
  • Current subject behaviour.
  • Members of the public nearby.
  • Positions of other officers.
  • Available distance and protection.
  • Access and exit routes.
  • Whether urgent life-saving action is required.
  • Whether further support is needed.

Exercise Four — Arrival Assessment

Position a roleplayer, response officer and member of the public within a controlled scene.

Students should arrive and verbally identify:

  • The subject or possible subject.
  • The current behaviour.
  • Whether a weapon is visible.
  • Who may be at risk.
  • Where other officers are positioned.
  • What immediate scene-control issue exists.
  • Whether urgent intervention is required.
  • What information should be communicated.

Do not assess advanced vehicle positioning or complex tactics during this exercise. Those are developed later.

Control Before Complexity

Reinforce that early incident management should establish what is happening and control the immediate risk.

Students should not rush into specialist tactics simply because they are armed officers.

Stage 6 — Early Scene Control

Early scene control may involve:

  • Identifying the immediate threat area.
  • Keeping members of the public away from danger.
  • Communicating with officers already present.
  • Establishing a safe working area.
  • Using available time and distance.
  • Requesting support for outer scene management.
  • Identifying safe medical access.
  • Maintaining observation and information flow.
  • Preserving options rather than forcing escalation.

Detailed armed-containment procedures are reserved for Module 9.

Stage 7 — Communications and APSTRA

A useful update should include:

Identity Officer, unit or ARV callsign.
Location Clear current location.
Incident What has been reported or observed.
Risk Who is at risk and why.
Action What the crew is currently doing.
Requirement Support, command or medical resources required.

Exercise Five — Initial APSTRA Update

Following the arrival exercise, require each student to deliver an individual APSTRA or radio update.

Assess whether the update:

  • Identifies the transmitting unit.
  • Gives a clear location.
  • Separates reports from observations.
  • Identifies current risk.
  • Explains the crew's immediate action.
  • Requests appropriate support.
  • Remains concise.
Example “ARV-2 at Legion Square. One male matching the description located near the reported vehicle. No weapon currently visible. High public presence. Establishing scene control and requesting additional units for public management.”

Radio Discipline

Correct students who provide long, repetitive updates containing no useful change.

Concise communication should not become vague communication. The update must still explain the current incident, risk and action.

Stage 8 — Command and Escalation

Command should be updated when:

  • The threat materially changes.
  • A weapon is confirmed or disproved.
  • The subject changes location.
  • Initial scene control succeeds or fails.
  • Further specialist resources are required.
  • Force is used.
  • A casualty is identified.
  • An urgent tactical decision is required.
  • The current plan is no longer suitable.

Students should understand that urgent action required to protect life must not be delayed solely because a commander is not yet present.

Exercise Six — Resource Request

Give students an incident requiring one or more supporting resources.

Require them to identify:

  • What resource is needed.
  • Why it is needed.
  • Where it should support.
  • What risk the request addresses.

Possible resources

  • Additional ARV.
  • Response officers.
  • Roads Policing.
  • NPAS.
  • K9.
  • Ambulance or medical support.
  • Firearms command.

Stage 9 — Working With Other Units

Response Officers May provide information, witness management and outer scene support.
Roads Policing May assist with vehicle management, traffic control and authorised pursuit activity.
NPAS May provide observation, tracking and wider situational awareness.
K9 May support searches, tracking and authorised specialist tasks.
Medical Support Provides casualty care once safe access is established.
Firearms Command Provides oversight, tactical direction and coordination where required.

Resource requests must explain what support is required and why.

Stage 10 — Changing Circumstances

Explain that students must change their assessment when relevant facts or behaviour change.

Material changes may include:

  • The subject begins complying.
  • The subject becomes more aggressive.
  • A weapon is confirmed.
  • A suspected item is disproved.
  • The subject enters a vehicle or building.
  • A hostage or vulnerable person is identified.
  • The public enters the scene.
  • Additional officers arrive.
  • A casualty requires urgent assistance.
  • Command direction changes.

Scenario One — Threat Reduces

Students arrive following a report that a male displayed a handgun. The male follows all directions, keeps his hands visible and moves away from an object on the ground.

Ask:

  • How has the immediate risk changed?
  • What control measures remain appropriate?
  • What actions may no longer be necessary?
  • What should be communicated?
  • What must happen regarding the object?

Expected learning points

  • Compliance may reduce the immediate threat.
  • The incident still requires safe control.
  • The response should reflect the reduced risk.
  • The object remains relevant and must be assessed safely.
  • APSTRA and command updates should reflect the change.

Scenario Two — Threat Escalates

During the incident, new information confirms a credible weapon and the subject begins threatening a nearby member of the public.

Ask:

  • What has changed?
  • Who is now at increased risk?
  • What must be communicated?
  • What support may be required?
  • Does the existing plan remain suitable?

Expected learning points

  • The information and behaviour have materially changed.
  • Risk to the public has increased.
  • The crew must reassess immediately.
  • Command and supporting units require an update.
  • Students should remain controlled rather than rushing blindly.

Scenario Three — Conflicting Information

A witness states that the reported handgun was a phone, while another caller continues to insist that it was a firearm.

Ask:

  • Does either statement automatically settle the incident?
  • What remains uncertain?
  • What further information could help?
  • How should the conflicting information be communicated?
  • What should happen to the current plan?

Expected learning points

  • Neither account automatically proves what the object was.
  • The crew should recognise and communicate the uncertainty.
  • Further assessment may be required.
  • Unnecessary escalation should be avoided.
  • The plan should remain flexible.

Stage 11 — Resolution and Handover

Once the immediate threat has been controlled, students should consider:

  • Subject status.
  • Weapons or suspected weapons located.
  • Medical requirements.
  • Updates to Force Control and firearms command.
  • Scene protection.
  • Witness identification.
  • Separation of involved persons.
  • Equipment accountability.
  • Required reports.

Life-saving care takes priority over evidence preservation.

Exercise Seven — Incident Handover

Following one of the scenarios, require each student to provide a brief handover covering:

  • Original report.
  • What the ARV crew observed.
  • Current subject status.
  • Weapons or relevant items.
  • Force used, if any.
  • Casualties or medical requirements.
  • Witnesses.
  • Outstanding risks.
  • Resources still required.

Assess accuracy, structure and honesty rather than dramatic presentation.

Stage 12 — Debrief

Debrief each student individually and then as a crew.

Ask:

  • What information did you receive?
  • What remained uncertain?
  • What did you communicate to your partner?
  • What did you identify on arrival?
  • What changed during the scenario?
  • How did your plan change?
  • What went well?
  • What would you improve?
  • What feedback will you apply next time?

Practical Assessment Standard

A satisfactory performance should demonstrate:

  • Professional preparation.
  • Effective crew communication.
  • Accurate handling of deployment information.
  • Recognition of information gaps.
  • Constructive use of travel time.
  • Controlled arrival assessment.
  • Recognition of risks to everyone involved.
  • Appropriate early scene-control decisions.
  • Clear APSTRA or radio updates.
  • Appropriate requests for support.
  • Recognition of changing circumstances.
  • Proportionate reassessment.
  • Clear incident handover.
  • Equipment accountability.
  • Constructive debrief participation.

Review Questions

  1. What is the role of an Armed Response Vehicle?
  2. How are Code44 ARVs normally crewed?
  3. What information should be obtained before arrival?
  4. Why must reported information be separated from confirmed information?
  5. How should travel time be used?
  6. What should be assessed on arrival?
  7. What is the purpose of early scene control?
  8. What information should an APSTRA update contain?
  9. When should command be updated?
  10. How can other departments support the incident?
  11. Why must the response change when circumstances change?
  12. What responsibilities remain after the immediate threat ends?
  13. What should students explain during the debrief?

Expected Answers

  1. To provide a mobile armed-response capability for spontaneous and planned incidents requiring trained armed support.
  2. Normally double-crewed by trained ARU officers.
  3. Incident, persons involved, location, weapon information, current behaviour, risks, available resources and important unknowns.
  4. Initial reports may be incomplete or inaccurate and should not be communicated as proven fact.
  5. To monitor updates, clarify information, identify risks, share responsibilities and prepare for arrival.
  6. Subject location and behaviour, weapon information, public and officer positions, available protection, access routes, urgent risks and resource requirements.
  7. To understand and manage the immediate risk, protect people and preserve safe options.
  8. Identity, location, incident, risk, current action and required support.
  9. Whenever the threat, weapon information, subject location, resources, casualties, force used or current plan materially changes.
  10. Response can support scene management, RPU can assist with roads and vehicles, NPAS can observe, K9 can support searches, medical resources can treat casualties and command can coordinate the response.
  11. Because new information or behaviour may increase or reduce the risk and make the original plan unsuitable.
  12. Medical care, command updates, scene protection, witness identification, handover, equipment accountability and reports.
  13. Information received, identified risks, decisions, communication, changes during the scenario, strengths and areas for improvement.

Immediate Stop Criteria

Stop the exercise immediately if a student:

  • Ignores a trainer safety command.
  • Continues while confused.
  • Acts outside the exercise boundary.
  • Displays reckless vehicle or firearms behaviour.
  • Uses specialist tactics not authorised within the module.
  • Fails to communicate a serious equipment or script problem.
  • Repeatedly disregards their crew partner.
  • Creates unnecessary risk to roleplayers or supporting officers.
  • Refuses trainer direction.

Module Outcomes

Pass

The student demonstrates satisfactory information handling, crew communication, arrival assessment, radio discipline, reassessment, handover and accountability.

Pass with Development Points

The student may progress but must address limited development areas identified during practical observation.

Remedial

The student requires further coaching or another controlled ARV-response scenario before progression.

Incomplete

Required reading, attendance, practical exercises or assessment remain outstanding.

Unsuccessful

The student has not demonstrated the required standard after appropriate training, feedback and reassessment.

Common Student Errors

  • Treating the initial report as confirmed fact.
  • Failing to identify information gaps.
  • Allowing one crew member to manage everything.
  • Failing to use travel time constructively.
  • Arriving with a fixed plan.
  • Ignoring members of the public or other officers.
  • Providing excessively long radio updates.
  • Requesting resources without explaining why.
  • Failing to reassess after a material change.
  • Rushing towards a tactical outcome.
  • Failing to provide an accurate handover.
  • Becoming defensive during the debrief.

Common Trainer Errors

  • Making the scenario too complex for the module stage.
  • Introducing hard stops, TPAC, breaching or CQB prematurely.
  • Giving students a fixed tactical answer.
  • Allowing one student to dominate every role.
  • Providing every piece of information without requiring questions.
  • Failing to change the scenario information.
  • Assessing drama or speed rather than control.
  • Failing to challenge unsupported assumptions.
  • Failing to assess each student individually.
  • Revealing expected answers before the debrief.
  • Failing to record development points.

Closing Script

Suggested wording

“That concludes Module 7.

You should now understand how a double-crewed ARV receives information, prepares while travelling, assesses an incident on arrival and supports the early management of an armed incident.

Strong ARV performance is not measured by how quickly you force an outcome.

It is measured by accurate information handling, communication, control, proportionate decisions and your ability to adapt when the circumstances change.

Module 8 will build upon these standards through armed vehicle tactics and coordinated vehicle-related incidents.”
