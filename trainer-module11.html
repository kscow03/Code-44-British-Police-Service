Trainer Module 11 — Complex Armed Incidents & Integrated Operations

Instructor Lesson Plan

ARU Academy • Cohort 001

Trainer-Only Resource

Lesson Overview

Module 11 consolidates the student's previous Academy learning through complex, multi-resource armed incidents.

This module introduces very little new theory. The main purpose is to assess whether students can combine the standards developed throughout Modules 1–10 while dealing with competing priorities, changing intelligence, multiple subjects, several locations and specialist resources.

Trainers should deliberately create pressure through information and changing circumstances rather than simply increasing the number of firearms or roleplayers involved.

Lesson Module 11 — Complex Armed Incidents & Integrated Operations
Recommended Duration 120–180 minutes
Delivery Style Briefing, integrated scenarios and structured debrief
Assessment Continuous practical assessment
Primary Focus Prioritisation, integration, communication and adaptability
Progression Required before Module 12

Training Boundary

Module 11 is designed for Code44 FiveM scenario training.

It should assess previously taught Code44 principles and server mechanics rather than introduce real-world specialist operational procedures.

Awareness of specialist capability does not qualify a student in precision firearms, breaching, Counter Terrorism, VIP protection, rope access, fast-rope or other specialist disciplines.

Trainer Preparation

  • Confirm students completed Module 10
  • Confirm Module 11 pre-learning has been completed
  • Prepare one simple integrated warm-up scenario
  • Prepare one full complex armed incident
  • Arrange roleplayers where available
  • Arrange response support where available
  • Arrange Roads Policing where appropriate
  • Arrange NPAS integration where available
  • Arrange K9 integration where available
  • Prepare medical or casualty injects
  • Prepare multiple-subject information
  • Prepare changing-location information
  • Prepare conflicting intelligence
  • Prepare command-direction changes
  • Prepare a communications challenge
  • Prepare an operational handover exercise
  • Prepare observation and assessment notes

Learning Outcomes

Students should demonstrate the ability to:

  • Identify the immediate operational priority.
  • Understand their assigned responsibility.
  • Remain aware of firearms command and the wider objective.
  • Process changing information without becoming overwhelmed.
  • Distinguish important information from unnecessary noise.
  • Work effectively with other police resources.
  • Use NPAS and K9 for appropriate operational purposes.
  • Recognise medical priorities.
  • Account for multiple subjects and locations.
  • Recognise competing operational priorities.
  • Communicate significant changes.
  • Reassess when circumstances materially change.
  • Transition appropriately as threats are resolved.
  • Provide a clear handover.
  • Explain decisions during debrief.

Suggested Lesson Structure

Stage Time Trainer Action
Introduction 10 minutes Explain that Module 11 assesses integration of previous learning.
Prioritisation Discussion 10 minutes Review how students identify immediate risks and assigned tasks.
Integrated Warm-Up 20 minutes Run a relatively simple multi-resource scenario.
Resource Integration 15 minutes Review ARU, response, RPU, NPAS, K9 and medical roles.

Opening Script

Suggested wording

“Module 11 is where we stop looking at individual skills in isolation.

You have already covered decision-making, equipment, firearms safety, ARV response, vehicle incidents, containment, searches and building operations.

Today I want to see whether you can still apply those standards when several things start happening at once.

Do not try to solve every problem personally. Understand your task, communicate, prioritise and use the resources around you.”

Stage 1 — Prioritisation

Students should understand that complex incidents may contain several legitimate priorities.

Ask students to continually consider:

  • What is the immediate threat?
  • Who is currently at greatest risk?
  • What is the wider operational objective?
  • What task have I been assigned?
  • What are other resources already managing?
  • What information needs escalating?
  • What can safely wait?

Exercise One — Competing Priorities Discussion

Scenario One armed suspect remains outstanding. A second suspect has been detained. A civilian has been injured. A vehicle linked to the incident has begun moving away. Response officers are requesting instructions.

Ask students:

  • What are the current problems?
  • Which one represents the greatest immediate risk?
  • Does one ARV need to solve every problem?
  • What other resources can be used?
  • What information does command need?

Expected learning points

  • Several priorities can exist simultaneously.
  • Tasks should be distributed appropriately.
  • Officers should remain focused on assigned critical responsibilities.
  • Communication and command coordination are essential.

Stage 2 — Command Awareness

Students should know who is coordinating the armed response and what their own responsibility is.

Command should receive relevant updates where:

  • The threat materially changes.
  • A new subject is identified.
  • A subject changes location.
  • A weapon is confirmed or disproved.
  • A casualty occurs.
  • Containment is established or lost.
  • A vehicle becomes relevant.
  • An assigned task cannot be completed.
  • A specialist resource is required.
  • The current plan is no longer suitable.

Stage 3 — Information Management

Trainers should deliberately provide students with more information than they need.

Students should identify what affects:

  • The current threat.
  • Their assigned role.
  • Subject location.
  • Public or officer safety.
  • Weapons.
  • Casualties.
  • The wider operational plan.
Trainer Prompt “Does that information actually change your task?”

Exercise Two — Information Overload

Provide several updates in quick succession.

For example:

  • A witness gives a new clothing description.
  • NPAS sees movement nearby.
  • A vehicle registration is provided.
  • Response requests assistance with a civilian.
  • A firearm is reportedly located.
  • A different subject is detained.

Assess whether students:

  • Identify what matters to their current task.
  • Communicate significant changes.
  • Avoid reacting unnecessarily to every update.
  • Recognise when clarification is required.

Stage 4 — Integrated Resources

ARU Armed response and specialist firearms capability.
Response Scene management, witnesses, evacuations and general policing support.
Roads Policing Vehicle incidents, traffic management and authorised pursuit support.
NPAS Observation, tracking and wider situational awareness.
K9 Search, tracking and authorised specialist support.
Medical Casualty management.

Students should be capable of explaining why a resource is required.

Exercise Three — Resource Selection

Give students several operational problems.

Examples:

  • A subject has fled into open terrain.
  • A vehicle connected to the incident is moving.
  • A large public cordon is required.
  • A casualty requires treatment.
  • An external overview of a location is required.

Ask students what resource could assist and, most importantly, why.

Stage 5 — NPAS Integration

Students should give NPAS useful operational tasks rather than vague requests.

Possible tasks include:

  • Observing a location.
  • Tracking a moving subject.
  • Tracking a vehicle.
  • Providing wider scene awareness.
  • Supporting a search.
Trainer Question “What exactly do you want NPAS to do for you?”

Stage 6 — K9 Integration

Students should identify:

  • What K9 is being requested to achieve.
  • What information the handler needs.
  • How the task fits into the wider operation.
  • What ARU responsibilities remain while K9 operates.

Scenario One — Subject Leaves Containment

An outstanding armed subject leaves the believed containment and enters a larger search area.

Ask:

  • What has changed?
  • Who needs to know?
  • What happens to the existing containment plan?
  • Could NPAS or K9 assist?
  • What responsibilities remain for ARU?

Stage 7 — Casualty Management

Casualties should change the operational picture but do not automatically remove an existing threat.

Students should consider:

  • Does the immediate threat remain?
  • Who needs treatment?
  • What medical capability exists?
  • Can another officer manage the casualty?
  • Can ambulance resources access safely within the scenario?
  • What information must be communicated?

Scenario Two — Officer Casualty

During an active incident, one officer becomes a simulated casualty while an armed subject remains outstanding.

Assess whether students:

  • Recognise both problems.
  • Communicate the casualty.
  • Consider available medical capability.
  • Avoid automatically abandoning every other operational responsibility.
  • Request appropriate support.
  • Reassess the wider plan.

Stage 8 — Multiple Subjects

Students should continually account for how many subjects are believed to be involved and how many have actually been controlled.

Trainer Prompt “We originally had three reported suspects. You have detained one. What happened to the other two?”

Scenario Three — Premature Resolution

Students successfully detain one armed subject.

The original briefing identified two possible additional subjects.

Assess whether students:

  • Remember the outstanding subjects.
  • Communicate that the incident remains active.
  • Avoid treating one arrest as complete resolution.
  • Reassess available information.

Stage 9 — Multiple Locations

Complex incidents may develop across different areas.

Students should consider:

  • Which location currently presents the greatest threat.
  • What resources are already allocated.
  • Whether both locations are genuinely connected.
  • Whether command needs separate resources or objectives.
  • Whether moving officers creates a new gap elsewhere.

Scenario Four — Second Location

During an active operation, intelligence identifies a second location linked to the incident.

Ask:

  • Does every officer immediately leave for the second location?
  • What remains unresolved at the first location?
  • Who needs the new information?
  • What additional resources may be required?
  • How should priorities be reassessed?

Stage 10 — Specialist Capabilities

Students should recognise the capabilities available within Code44 ARU without treating them as automatic solutions.

SIG 716 Specialist precision capability where authorised.
Remington Specialist breaching capability.
Ballistic Shield Specialist protective capability.
Counter Terrorism Specialist operational capability where authorised.
VIP Protection Protective capability where required.
Specialist Access Rope access, fast-rope or other server-supported capabilities.

Capability Does Not Equal Requirement

If a student requests a specialist capability, ask:

“What operational problem are you trying to solve?”

If they cannot explain the need, the request may not be justified.

Stage 11 — Complex Scenario

The main Module 11 scenario should combine several previously learned elements.

A suitable scenario may involve:

  • An initial firearms deployment.
  • A moving vehicle.
  • A building or containment element.
  • Multiple subjects.
  • At least one civilian.
  • NPAS or K9 availability.
  • A casualty.
  • Conflicting intelligence.
  • A changing operational objective.

Do not include every possible complication simply to make the scenario difficult.

Every inject should test a specific learning point.

Example Complex Scenario

Initial Briefing Police receive reports of three masked individuals involved in an armed robbery. The group leaves in a dark SUV. ARV and Roads Policing locate a vehicle matching the description. A firearm has been reported but not yet confirmed.

Possible Trainer Injects

  1. The vehicle stops and two subjects run towards separate areas.
  2. A third subject remains with the vehicle.
  3. NPAS becomes available.
  4. One subject enters a building.
  5. A civilian is discovered inside.
  6. Another subject is believed to have entered open ground.
  7. K9 becomes available.
  8. One simulated casualty occurs.
  9. New information confirms a firearm connected with one subject.
  10. Command changes one operational priority.

Trainers should introduce injects gradually.

What To Assess During Complex Scenarios

Prioritisation Identifies immediate risks and important tasks.
Command Awareness Understands the wider objective and command direction.
Information Management Processes significant information without becoming overwhelmed.
Communication Passes relevant updates clearly.
Role Discipline Maintains assigned responsibilities unless change is required.
Resource Integration Uses other units for clear operational purposes.
Subject Accountability Maintains awareness of outstanding subjects.
Medical Awareness Recognises casualties and available support.
Reassessment Updates decisions when information changes.
Professional Control Remains calm when the scenario becomes complicated.

Stage 12 — Transition & Resolution

Students should understand that parts of the incident may resolve at different times.

Possible transitions include:

  • Vehicle phase into armed containment.
  • Containment into search.
  • Arrest into ongoing search for outstanding subjects.
  • Threat management into casualty care.
  • Armed operation into investigation.

Students should continually ask what threat or responsibility remains outstanding.

Exercise Four — Operational Handover

After the main scenario, require each student to provide a handover.

The handover should cover:

  • Original incident.
  • Subjects originally reported.
  • Subjects accounted for.
  • Outstanding subjects.
  • Weapon information.
  • Vehicles.
  • Relevant locations.
  • Casualties.
  • Force used if relevant.
  • Outstanding risks.
  • Resources still deployed.
  • Further action required.

Stage 13 — Debrief

Debrief students individually before discussing the scenario as a team.

Ask:

  • What was your immediate priority?
  • What task were you assigned?
  • What information mattered most?
  • What information did you disregard and why?
  • What changed during the incident?
  • How did your decisions change?
  • What resource did you request and why?
  • Did you lose awareness of anything?
  • What went well?
  • What would you improve?

Module 11 Assessment Standard

A satisfactory student should demonstrate:

  • Professional control under increased workload.
  • Effective prioritisation.
  • Accurate information handling.
  • Clear communication.
  • Awareness of command.
  • Role discipline.
  • Effective use of other police resources.
  • Awareness of multiple subjects and locations.
  • Recognition of casualties.
  • Continuous reassessment.
  • Ability to adapt without becoming disorganised.
  • Clear operational handover.
  • Honest debrief and accountability.

Review Questions

  1. What makes an armed incident complex?
  2. How should you identify operational priorities?
  3. Why is command awareness important?
  4. How should officers manage information overload?
  5. Why should officers avoid trying to solve every problem personally?
  6. What can NPAS contribute?
  7. What can K9 contribute?
  8. How can a casualty change operational priorities?
  9. Why must officers continue accounting for outstanding subjects?
  10. When should specialist capabilities be requested?
  11. What should happen when the operational picture changes?
  12. What information should be included within the final handover?

Expected Answers

  1. Multiple subjects, locations, resources, threats, casualties, conflicting intelligence or competing priorities.
  2. Consider the immediate threat, who is at greatest risk, the wider objective and the officer's assigned responsibility.
  3. Because individual actions must support the wider operation and significant changes may require command decisions.
  4. Focus on information relevant to the threat, assigned task, safety and operational plan.
  5. Other resources may already be better placed to manage different problems, and abandoning an important task may create additional risk.
  6. Observation, tracking, vehicle tracking, search support and wider situational awareness.
  7. Search, tracking and other authorised specialist support.
  8. Medical care may become an additional priority while the wider threat still requires management.
  9. Controlling one subject does not automatically resolve the wider incident.
  10. When they address a specific operational problem and are appropriate to the objective.
  11. Reassess, communicate and alter tasks or plans as required.
  12. Subjects, weapons, vehicles, locations, casualties, outstanding risks, deployed resources and further action.

Immediate Stop Criteria

Stop or reset the scenario if a student:

  • Ignores trainer safety instructions.
  • Becomes completely confused and continues regardless.
  • Acts outside the scenario boundary.
  • Repeatedly abandons assigned responsibilities without communication.
  • Fails to communicate major threat changes.
  • Repeatedly loses awareness of other officers or civilians.
  • Uses specialist capabilities contrary to trainer direction.
  • Ignores a significant server issue.
  • Displays reckless firearms or vehicle behaviour.
  • Refuses trainer direction.

Module Outcomes

Pass

Student demonstrates satisfactory performance across complex integrated scenarios and can combine previous Academy learning effectively.

Pass with Development Points

Student may progress but has specific areas to refine during Module 12.

Remedial

Student requires additional integrated scenarios before progression.

Incomplete

Required practical work or assessment remains outstanding.

Unsuccessful

Student has not demonstrated sufficient ability to combine previous Academy learning after appropriate training and reassessment.

Common Student Errors

  • Trying to personally solve every problem.
  • Abandoning their assigned task whenever new radio traffic appears.
  • Failing to prioritise.
  • Becoming overwhelmed by information.
  • Requesting every specialist resource available.
  • Forgetting outstanding subjects after an arrest.
  • Ignoring casualties.
  • Failing to update command.
  • Continuing with an outdated plan.
  • Providing excessive radio traffic.
  • Losing awareness of the wider incident.

Common Trainer Errors

  • Making complexity equal chaos.
  • Introducing too many injects at once.
  • Creating complications with no learning purpose.
  • Failing to provide a clear initial objective.
  • Expecting students to command the entire incident.
  • Failing to integrate other units realistically.
  • Assessing drama instead of decision-making.
  • Not allowing students time to process significant information.
  • Failing to assess each student individually.
  • Skipping the handover or debrief.

Closing Script

Suggested wording

“That concludes Module 11.

At this point in the Academy you already have most of the individual skills we expect you to know.

The challenge is now whether you can combine them when the incident becomes complicated.

You do not need to solve everything yourself. Stay controlled, understand your task, communicate, use the resources around you and reassess when circumstances change.

Module 12 will consolidate everything you have learned and prepare you for final assessment.”
