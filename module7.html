Module 7 — ARV Deployment & Armed Incident Response

Code44 Police Service

Armed Response Unit Academy

Cohort 001

Module Overview

Module 7 introduces students to deployment as part of a double-crewed Armed Response Vehicle.

Students will learn how an ARV crew receives information, prepares for deployment, communicates while travelling, arrives at an incident, supports initial scene control and provides clear updates to Force Control and firearms command.

This module focuses on safe and accountable incident response. Specialist vehicle-interception tactics, building-entry methods and advanced armed operations are taught separately in later modules.

Training Phase Operational Deployment
Delivery Trainer-led classroom and practical scenarios
Assessment Continuous practical observation and knowledge check
Progression Required before Module 8

Module Boundary

Module 7 teaches the principles of responding to and managing developing armed incidents within Code44 roleplay.

It does not qualify students in TPAC, hard stops, smash-and-grab tactics, dynamic building entry, breaching or specialist firearms roles.

Students must follow trainer instructions, current Code44 policy and the scenario boundaries provided during every practical exercise.

1. Learning Objectives

By the end of Module 7, students should be able to:

  • Explain the role of a Code44 Armed Response Vehicle.
  • Describe the responsibilities shared by a double-crewed ARV.
  • Identify important information required before deployment.
  • Recognise information gaps and ask appropriate questions.
  • Maintain communication while travelling to an incident.
  • Conduct an initial assessment on arrival.
  • Recognise the importance of using distance, time and available protection.
  • Provide a concise APSTRA update.
  • Request appropriate support.
  • Work professionally with response, RPU, NPAS, K9 and medical resources.
  • Recognise when circumstances have changed.
  • Update the plan and command when required.
  • Understand post-incident handover and accountability.

2. Role of the Armed Response Vehicle

The ARV provides a mobile armed-response capability for spontaneous and planned incidents.

ARV crews may be deployed to:

  • Reports involving firearms or suspected firearms.
  • High-risk incidents requiring trained armed support.
  • Incidents where officers or members of the public may face an immediate threat.
  • Developing incidents requiring containment and specialist assessment.
  • Planned operations authorised by firearms command.
  • Incidents where specialist equipment or medical capability may be required.

The presence of an ARV does not automatically determine how an incident will be resolved.

The crew must still gather information, assess risk, communicate and use proportionate options.

Double-Crewed Capability

Code44 ARVs are normally double-crewed by trained ARU officers.

Both officers are responsible for supporting the deployment. One officer should not carry the entire information, communication or decision-making workload while the other remains passive.

3. Information Before Deployment

The initial information received may be incomplete, inaccurate or changing.

Students should identify:

What Has Happened? The reported incident and reason armed support has been requested.
Who Is Involved? Suspects, victims, witnesses, officers and vulnerable persons.
Where Is It Happening? Exact location, direction of travel and relevant access information.
What Weapon Is Reported? Whether a weapon has been seen, described, confirmed or only suspected.
What Is the Current Behaviour? What the subject is doing now, rather than only what happened earlier.
Who Is at Risk? Members of the public, officers, hostages, victims or the subject.
What Resources Are Present? Response officers, RPU, NPAS, K9, ambulance or other ARVs.
What Remains Unknown? Important gaps that may affect the response.

Initial Reports Are Not Automatically Confirmed Facts

A caller reporting a firearm provides important information, but the crew must continue assessing what is known, observed, confirmed and still uncertain.

Students must not add details to a report or communicate assumptions as though they have been confirmed.

4. ARV Crew Responsibilities

Exact responsibilities may change depending upon the incident, vehicle position, information and command direction.

The crew should nevertheless establish a shared understanding before arrival.

Driver Maintains safe vehicle operation, listens to updates and positions the vehicle in accordance with the developing situation and trainer or command direction.
Passenger / Crew Partner Supports navigation, monitors radio information, updates the driver and helps prepare the crew for arrival.
Contact Responsibility Where assigned, communicates with the subject, witnesses, officers or Force Control.
Cover and Awareness Where assigned, maintains wider awareness and communicates changes affecting the crew.

These responsibilities are not rigid titles that prevent communication. Both officers remain accountable for what they observe and do.

5. Travelling to the Incident

Travel time should be used to prepare rather than simply waiting to arrive.

The crew should:

  • Listen to radio updates.
  • Confirm the location.
  • Clarify the subject description.
  • Identify whether a weapon is confirmed or suspected.
  • Discuss known risks.
  • Identify vulnerable persons.
  • Consider what other units are present.
  • Confirm which radio channel is being used.
  • Discuss immediate priorities.
  • Recognise when information changes.
Crew Question “What do we know, what do we not know, and what is our immediate priority when we arrive?”

6. Arrival and Initial Assessment

The crew should avoid arriving with a fixed plan that ignores what is actually happening.

On arrival, students should assess:

  • The current location of the subject.
  • Whether the subject matches the information provided.
  • Whether a weapon is visible or confirmed.
  • Current subject behaviour.
  • Members of the public in the area.
  • Positions of other officers.
  • Available distance and protection.
  • Access and exit routes.
  • Whether urgent life-saving action is required.
  • Whether additional resources are needed.

Students should communicate important observations rather than assuming their partner or Force Control has seen the same thing.

Control Before Complexity

Early incident management should focus on understanding and controlling the immediate risk.

Students should not rush into specialist tactics simply because ARU has arrived.

Time, communication, distance and containment may provide a safer and more proportionate response where immediate action is not required.

7. Initial Scene Control

Initial scene control may involve:

  • Identifying the immediate threat area.
  • Preventing members of the public entering danger.
  • Communicating with officers already present.
  • Establishing a safe working area.
  • Using available time and distance.
  • Requesting unarmed officers to support outer scene control where appropriate.
  • Identifying safe access for medical support.
  • Maintaining observation and information flow.
  • Preserving options rather than forcing unnecessary escalation.

Detailed armed-containment tactics are covered in Module 9. Module 7 introduces the purpose and principles of early scene control only.

8. Communications and APSTRA

Communications should help Force Control, other officers and firearms command understand what is happening.

A useful update should include:

Identity Officer, unit or ARV callsign.
Location Clear current location.
Incident What has been reported or observed.
Risk Who may be at risk and why.
Action What the crew is currently doing.
Requirement Resources, command or medical support required.
Example APSTRA Update “ARV-2 at Legion Square. One male matching the description located near the reported vehicle. No weapon currently visible. High public presence. Establishing scene control and requesting additional units for public management.”

Updates should be refreshed when the threat, location, subject behaviour, available information or tactical plan changes.

Do Not Flood the Radio

Radio updates should be relevant, clear and timely.

Students should avoid long, repetitive transmissions that prevent other officers from passing urgent information.

9. Command and Escalation

Students should understand when firearms command or supervisory direction may be required.

Command should be updated when:

  • The threat materially changes.
  • A weapon is confirmed or disproved.
  • The subject moves location.
  • Containment is established or fails.
  • Additional specialist resources are required.
  • Force is used.
  • A casualty is identified.
  • An urgent tactical decision is required.
  • The current plan is no longer suitable.

Officers should not delay immediate action genuinely required to protect life solely because a commander is not yet present.

Command must be updated as soon as reasonably possible.

10. Working With Other Units

ARU exists to support the wider police service, not operate separately from it.

Response Officers May provide initial information, witnesses, scene control and support away from the immediate threat area.
Roads Policing May support vehicle containment, traffic management and pursuit activity within their authorised role.
NPAS May provide aerial observation, subject tracking and wider situational awareness.
K9 May support searches, tracking and specialist operational tasks when directed.
Ambulance / Medical Support Provides casualty care when safe access has been established.
Firearms Command Provides command oversight, tactical direction and coordination where required.

Requests should explain what support is needed and why.

11. Changing Circumstances

Students must remain capable of adjusting the response as the incident develops.

Examples of material change include:

  • The subject begins complying.
  • The subject becomes more aggressive.
  • A reported weapon is located.
  • The suspected item is shown not to be a weapon.
  • The subject enters a building or vehicle.
  • A hostage or vulnerable person is identified.
  • Members of the public enter the scene.
  • Additional officers arrive.
  • Medical urgency increases.
  • Command direction changes.
Reassessment Question “What has changed, what does that mean for the risk, and does our current plan remain appropriate?”

A Controlled Resolution Is a Successful Resolution

Students should not measure success by whether a firearm was deployed or force was used.

Good ARV performance may involve controlling the scene, gathering better information, reducing risk, supporting communication and resolving the incident without further escalation.

12. Resolution and Handover

Once the immediate threat has been controlled, the crew must transition into post-incident responsibilities.

These may include:

  • Confirming the subject's status.
  • Confirming whether weapons have been located.
  • Providing or requesting medical care.
  • Updating Force Control and firearms command.
  • Protecting the scene.
  • Identifying witnesses.
  • Separating involved persons where appropriate.
  • Providing a clear handover.
  • Accounting for ARU equipment.
  • Completing required reports.

Life-saving care takes priority over evidence preservation.

13. Debrief and Accountability

Students should participate honestly in the post-exercise debrief.

They should be able to explain:

  • What information they received.
  • What they believed the threat to be.
  • What remained unknown.
  • What they communicated to their partner.
  • What decisions they made.
  • What changed during the scenario.
  • How their response changed.
  • What went well.
  • What they would improve.
  • What equipment or server issues occurred.

Honest reflection is part of operational competence. Students should not attempt to create a perfect answer after the event.

Practical Training

Module 7 includes several trainer-led ARV response scenarios.

Students may be assessed on:

  • Pre-deployment information gathering.
  • Crew communication.
  • Radio discipline.
  • Arrival assessment.
  • Recognition of public and officer risk.
  • Initial scene-control decisions.
  • APSTRA updates.
  • Requests for support.
  • Response to changing circumstances.
  • Post-incident handover.
  • Debrief participation.

Hidden scenario information and trainer assessment guidance are not included within the student material.

What Trainers Are Looking For

Trainers are not looking for students to rush into the incident or force a dramatic outcome.

Students should demonstrate:

  • Professional preparation.
  • Clear communication.
  • Shared crew responsibility.
  • Controlled decision-making.
  • Accurate information handling.
  • Awareness of wider risks.
  • Appropriate requests for assistance.
  • Reassessment.
  • Accountability.

14. Module 7 Revision

Before completing the knowledge check, students should be comfortable answering the following:

  1. What is the purpose of an Armed Response Vehicle?
  2. How are Code44 ARVs normally crewed?
  3. What information should the crew obtain before arrival?
  4. Why must reported information be separated from confirmed information?
  5. What should the crew discuss while travelling to the incident?
  6. What should officers assess when they first arrive?
  7. Why are time, communication and distance important?
  8. What information should be included within an APSTRA update?
  9. When should firearms command be updated?
  10. How can response, RPU, NPAS and K9 support an incident?
  11. Why must the tactical plan change when circumstances change?
  12. What responsibilities remain after the immediate threat ends?
  13. What should a student explain during the debrief?

Progression Standard

Module 7 is complete when the student has:

  • Completed the Module 7 student learning
  • Attended the trainer-led lesson
  • Completed the ARV crew briefing exercise
  • Demonstrated effective crew communication
  • Delivered a clear radio or APSTRA update
  • Completed an arrival-assessment exercise
  • Recognised public, officer and subject risks
  • Requested appropriate support
  • Responded to changing scenario information
  • Completed a post-incident handover
  • Participated properly in the debrief
  • Completed the Module 7 knowledge check
  • Received trainer sign-off
